**Example 6: Low-Cost Redirect Lever Invention — Midstream Intermodal Reroute & Origin-Side Flip**

**When a mid-2026 Finance/ATS trailer-cap directive tightened the redirect-eligibility distance gate (1,000→750 miles) and cut redirect volume ~12%, William built new, lower-cost redirect levers that recover the freight at points in the transportation cycle where Amazon pays only incremental cost.** Redirect execution had effectively one mechanism: a full downstream re-move once freight reached a stressed node. William looked for cheaper intervention points at both ends of the load lifecycle:

- **AZIM 1P intermodal midstream reroute (in-transit).** Rather than wait for an intermodal load to arrive and then pay a full downstream redirect, William reroutes it *while it is still on the rail leg* to a taker that needs the work — Amazon pays only the difference between the re-pointed final-mile dray and the original one, and avoids the full giver→taker over-the-road re-move entirely. He wrote the reroutability logic himself — rail-tier, steel-wheel-exclusion, standard-vs-crosstown (the divert window closes when a load departs its rail-interchange crosstown), and chassis-zone gates that determine whether an in-transit load can still be legally and physically diverted — and set up the request-and-execution process with the Amazon Intermodal (AZIM) team. Since launching in WK24 the lever has redirected **323 in-transit loads / 5.39M units (~46/week)** for a **net transport avoidance of ~$137K to date — ~$820K on an annualized basis (scaled to the seasonal redirect-volume curve, not a flat run-rate)** — priced load-by-load against carrier settlement actuals, with the avoided rail continuation on ramp-change flips measured from its own canceled rail leg rather than estimated (conservative floor ~$109K if those canceled legs were somehow still billed; current-week in-flight loads remain estimated pending accrual). The mechanism at its best: 28 loads bound for a Carolina rail ramp were intercepted at the Chicago crosstown and trucked straight to the Toledo taker — so the re-pointed load runs a shorter rail structure (origin→Chicago instead of origin→Carolina) *and* skips the downstream truck move, on every load. **The same midstream principle carries to 3P intermodal (HJBI/PGLI) loads**, where an in-transit rail reroute likewise substitutes an incremental leg for a full OTR redirect.
- **OB NTI origin-side flip (pre-dispatch).** At the opposite end of the cycle, William drove the outbound New-Transfer-In flip — redirecting transfer freight at the *origin* before a truck ever dispatches (nIXD → rIXD FC2), the cheapest possible intervention point — from an inconsistent break-glass action into a documented standard lever, reaching agreement with Reactive Scheduling and Amazon Transportation Services (ATS) on the mechanics and eligibility.
- **TOFC over OTR — mechanism choice, executed.** He backs lever selection with unit economics: the LAS1 Trailer-on-Flatcar (TOFC) teardown priced the same freight at **$2.6K/trailer railed via TOFC vs $6.6K over-the-road — ~$4K/trailer saved by railing it instead of trucking it** (and ~$1.7K/trailer below the incumbent LAS1 routing). The Q2 LAS1 TOFC program adopted this routing, choosing the cheapest feasible path per lane rather than defaulting to OTR.

Told to move less, William found ways to move the same freight for less money — and wrote each lever's eligibility rules so the team runs them as standard levers rather than re-deriving them per load.
